THE CHALLENGE

The Doctor's Dilemma

A disease affects 10% of the population.

A test has 90% accuracy (for both positive and negative).

You test positive. What's the probability you're actually sick?

Drag to make your guess:

50%
0% 100%
Most people guess around 80-90%. Let's see why they're wrong...
KEY INSIGHT

Think in Areas

Imagine 1000 people. Let's sort them:

Healthy (900)

810 test negative ✓

90 false positives ✗

Sick (100)

90 true positives ✓

10 false negatives ✗

False positive True positive

Who tests positive?

90 sick + 90 healthy = 180 total

Of these, only 90 are actually sick

P(sick | positive) = 90/180 = 50%

CHECK UNDERSTANDING

Why did most people guess wrong?

What crucial information did they ignore?

The test accuracy (90%)
The base rate of disease (10%)
The number of people tested
The age of patients

The base rate (prior probability) dramatically affects the result. With only 10% disease prevalence, even a 90% accurate test produces many false positives.

THE FORMULA

Bayes' Theorem

P(A|B) = P(B|A) × P(A) / P(B)
P(B|A)
Likelihood
P(A)
Prior
P(A|B)
Posterior

In our medical example:

P(sick | test+) = P(test+ | sick) × P(sick) / P(test+)

= 0.9 × 0.1 / 0.18

= 0.5

Core insight: Your prior belief matters just as much as the evidence.

CHECK UNDERSTANDING

What's the key difference?

Between MLE and MAP?

MLE incorporates prior knowledge, MAP doesn't
MAP adds prior knowledge to prevent overfitting
They always give the same answer
MLE is always better with more data

MLE: argmax P(data | θ)

MAP: argmax P(data | θ) × P(θ)

KEY REVELATION

Regularization = Prior

L2 Regularization
loss + λ||w||²
↕
Gaussian prior
L1 Regularization
loss + λ|w|
↕
Laplace prior
Practical: When you add regularization, you're saying "I believe weights should be small." That's a Bayesian prior.
